Getting there & getting down
Car shuttle. Leave a car in the Sandia Peak Tramway's base parking lot, then drive to the La Luz Trailhead near the Juan Tabo Picnic Area to start. Starting there instead of at the tram's base saves about 1.7 miles each way; to skip the shuttle, see the Optional card at the top. A day-use parking pass is required at the La Luz Trailhead; the New Mexico Enchantment Pass and Interagency passes are accepted.

Plan A — ride the tram down to the car at the tram's base. The route ends at the tram's upper terminal, and hike-up, ride-down is a standard option the tram explicitly supports, with one-way tickets (about $24 at last check). Sandia Peak Tramway confirmed by phone on Oct 3 that you can buy one-way tickets down at the top, last minute, on Friday — no need to buy online ahead of time, despite the Balloon Fiesta crowds. Getting people off the mountain is a priority for them, but there may be a wait of a few hours for a car down. The tram also runs weather permitting; high winds can shut it down. Tickets & hours at sandiapeak.com.

Plan B — hike back down La Luz to the car at the La Luz Trailhead. The charts on this page model this full out-and-back; see the Down card at the top for how the finish compares to sunset. With the optional start, plan B means retracing the Tramway Trail to the parking lot too, which runs past sunset (see Totals and Daylight).

Plan C — hike down to the 10K Trailhead for a pickup. If the tram falls through and nobody's up for the whole descent, take the King of the Mountain Trail to the 10K Trail (South) and down to the 10K Trailhead parking lot on NM 536, where you can get a ride if you need one. Those trails are open, and the 10K north lot is fully open with restrooms (as of Oct 3), so a driver can park there and wait.
Water & sun
There's no water anywhere along La Luz itself. You can refill at Ten 3, the restaurant at the top, as long as the tram is running — it closes in high winds, and Ten 3 may not be an option then — so carry enough for the climb plus a reserve (see Energy & Hydration above). The lower half climbs through open, south- and west-facing desert scrub with very little shade (as is the Tramway Trail, if you take the optional start), and if you hike back down you'll come through it in the late afternoon, when the lower switchbacks can be far warmer than the forecast at the top suggests. UV peaks around midday, while you're on the upper climb (and getting the most of the altitude boost shown in the UV chart), and the cool air up there makes it easy to forget — a sun hoodie and visor, sunscreen, and sunglasses.
Terrain & pace
La Luz is well-marked and easy to follow, averaging about a 9% grade with few level spots to catch your breath. Long stretches are loose decomposed granite, and higher up the trail crosses a talus field known as “the rockslide,” where the footing is difficult. The upper sections are narrow, alongside a steep drop. The last mile or so traverses south along the upper slopes to the tram terminal, at about 10,100–10,300 ft. Stay on the switchbacks rather than cutting corners.

Coming down is easier on the lungs but harder on the legs. In one lab study, peak knee-joint force walking down a 24° slope was about 7× body weight, versus about 4.6× walking up the same slope (Schwameder et al.). The quads also do braking work that builds fatigue and soreness over a long descent. Trekking poles cut knee loads by about 12–25%. Loose rock and talus slow a descent more than any pace formula predicts, so budget extra time if you hike down.
Cold, wind & altitude at the top
The route rises about 3,260 ft from the trailhead (7,026 ft) to the tram's upper terminal (10,290 ft) — about 3,630 ft of total climbing once the ups and downs along the way are counted. Expect the top to be noticeably colder and windier than the trailhead, and October can bring the first snow or ice to the shaded upper switchbacks — if a storm passes through in the days before, check recent AllTrails reviews for conditions. Altitude can also make the upper climb feel harder than the numbers suggest, especially for anyone coming from low elevation.
Daylight
On Oct 9, sunrise is around 7:07 AM and sunset around 6:38 PM in Albuquerque. If the day runs long, the descent is the worst place to lose the light — bring a headlamp.
Wildlife
Rattlesnakes are possible on the warm, rocky lower trail and the foothill Tramway Trail, even in early October on a sunny day — watch where you put your hands and feet, and skip the headphones so you'll hear a rattle. Ankle-high boots and long trekking pants help: a hiker who steps near a snake is most exposed at the foot, ankle, and lower leg, and even a layer of fabric between fang and skin substantially cuts the venom delivered. Trekking poles help too — planted ahead of you, their vibration tends to warn snakes off, and they keep a bit of distance between you and anything coiled beside the trail. If someone is bitten, per the CDC: call 911, keep calm and still, remove rings or watches before swelling starts, and don't cut, suck, ice, or tourniquet the bite. Black bears live in the Sandias too; don't leave food unattended at the top.
Trail status & closures
La Luz is in the Sandia Mountain Wilderness, managed by the Sandia Ranger District of Cibola National Forest. The Sandia Crest area itself is closed for a Forest Service project through Jan 15, 2027 — including the Crest Spur from La Luz to the Crest House and NM 536 beyond the 10K Trailhead — but La Luz up to the tram, King of the Mountain, and the 10K Trail (South) are open, so the route and all three plans for getting down are unaffected. Cell coverage is poor along much of La Luz, so don't count on a phone signal for calls. Check Cibola NF alerts before heading out for any fire restrictions, closures, or trail damage, and check sandiapeak.com for the tram's status the morning of.
